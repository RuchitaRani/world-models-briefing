01 · The idea

A world model answers one question:
"if we do this, what happens next?"

The survey writes a world model as a parameterised system that approximates the environment's dynamics:

the next latent state: in the survey's words, a summary of "the information necessary for predicting the future". For us: where the cup is and how it is moving.
the next observation: the camera frame we expect to see
the reward: a number the task designer defines (explained below)
the action: the push we are considering
the network's learned parameters

Is the reward always predicted? No.

In POMDP terms, a world model approximates the transition function T, and "optionally" the reward function R and the observation model O. Whether a model has a reward head depends on what it is for:

So Eq.1 is the general form. Real models keep the parts they need: MuZero drops , video generators drop .

What "full state" means, and why we need a belief

The survey: in fully observed settings the latent state can be the true environment state; under partial observability the model must infer a "belief-like" state from the interaction history.

So before predicting anything, the model has to work out the current state from what it has seen so far. This is the survey's Eq.2:

the encoder: a network that reads the history and outputs a probability distribution over possible states
"sampled from": we are not certain of the state, so it is a distribution, and we draw from it
the history
every camera frame up to and including now
every action taken before now

Why history fills the gaps

Velocity: two consecutive frames show how far the cup moved.
Mass and friction: how far the cup slid after our last push tells us how heavy or sticky it is.
Hidden objects: something visible three frames ago is probably still there.

And the reward

The reward is not something the world model invents; the task defines it. For our task a natural choice is the negative distance between cup and target, so closer means higher:

My example. In model-based RL the model learns to predict this number from the latent state (Eq.8, ).

Imagining, then optimising inside the imagination

Applying the model to its own output gives an imagined rollout (Eq.3), and we can optimise the policy entirely inside it (Eq.4):

the policy: which push to make in each state
the imagination horizon: how many steps ahead we roll
discount between 0 and 1: rewards further ahead count a little less
total reward the policy would earn inside the model
The survey's phrase for what a good world model does is "predictive abstraction": compress experience into something simple enough to simulate yet rich enough to act on.

The three properties the survey uses to separate a world model from a generic predictor

1 · Action-conditioning

"The model predicts how the environment evolves in response to specific actions, enabling counterfactual reasoning."

2 · Multi-step rollout

"The model can be applied autoregressively to generate trajectories of arbitrary length, supporting planning and simulation."

3 · Utility for decision-making

Predictions are "used downstream—for policy optimization, planning, data augmentation, or safety verification", not as an end in themselves.

Talk notes
Point at each coloured symbol and map it to the cup. Stress that the reward is optional: video world models like Sora or Genie have none, JEPA planners use distance to a goal image instead. Then the belief: one photo can't show speed or weight, so the encoder reads the whole history. J(π) is "how much reward the policy would get if it lived inside our model".
02 · What's inside one

Five parts, one loop

Click through the steps. The dynamics model is the heart of it; the survey calls it "the core of a world model".

Why not just learn a policy directly?

That's model-free RL (DQN, PPO, SAC). It works, but it needs huge amounts of real interaction. Learning how the world works first is far more data-efficient: PlaNet reported about 50× better data efficiency than model-free methods, and PILCO learned cart-pole swing-up from 17.5 seconds of experience.

The price is model bias. If the model is wrong somewhere, the policy will find that spot and exploit it. This comes back as bottleneck #2.

Not everyone keeps the decoder

Dreamer reconstructs frames. MuZero and JEPA don't: they never draw a picture, they only predict what's needed for decisions. Whether to predict pixels or not is the single choice that splits the field, and it's the thread I'll follow through the architecture section.

Talk notes
Go through the five step buttons slowly. For the cup: encoder squeezes a 196,608-number frame into a vector of maybe 32–256 numbers; dynamics predicts where the cup goes after a push; reward head says if that's good; policy picks the push; and the purple loop is the "dream". Then: "the decoder is optional, and that choice matters later".
03 · Two ways to classify any world model

What does it output?
And what does it think in?

Function: what comes out

Renderer: outputs pixels meant for human eyes, for example the next video frame of a game world (Genie 3, Sora).

Simulator: outputs a state that is geometrically, physically or dynamically faithful, so other programs can compute on it or train agents inside it (Dreamer's latent world, Marble's 3D scenes, Cosmos used as a training environment).

Planner: outputs actions, meaning the next push or a whole sequence of pushes. A planner gets there in one of two ways. It can search: imagine many candidate action sequences with the model, score each imagined future, and return the best first action, then replan (MPC with CEM or MPPI in TD-MPC2 and V-JEPA 2-AC, tree search in MuZero). Or it can generate the action directly as part of its prediction, as world action models like DreamZero and Cosmos Policy do. In the roadmap's agent loop, the simulator corresponds to the transition model, the renderer to the observation model, and the planner chooses actions over the belief state.

Architecture: what's inside

Observation-level (predicts pixels, tokens or text), latent (a learned compact state), or 3D/structured (occupancy, Gaussians, objects, graphs).

One subtle point from the Shanghai roadmap: a "world action model" is a function (planner), not a new architecture. It shows up in all three columns.

The phases are not one line: four lineages run in parallel

The phases describe what dominated each period, but the field has four separate families of ideas that run side by side and sometimes merge. JEPA is one of them. It is not part of the token-and-scale trend in phase 3; it began in 2022 as a reaction against it.

Positions are by year. DreamZero marks the point where the pixel/token line starts outputting actions (world action models). The curve at the top right marks the 2026 move to omni backbones such as Cosmos 3 and Atlas, which take on several roles at once.
Where JEPA fits. LeCun proposed it in 2022 because he considered predicting every pixel or token wasteful. It belongs to the latent family, like Dreamer, but it is non-generative: no decoder, it predicts embeddings rather than frames or tokens. Its milestones span phases 3–5: JEPA proposal (2022) → I-JEPA on images (2023) → V-JEPA on video (2024) → V-JEPA 2-AC planning on real robots (2025) → LeWM trained end-to-end (2026).

07 · Architecture families

The real question is: what do we predict?

Every family is an answer to that. Left means more detail and more cost; right means more abstraction and less to look at.

The survey organises methods into recurrent/state-space, transformer, diffusion, physics-informed and language families, and representations into pixels, continuous latents, tokens, joint embeddings, objects and 3D. I merged both views into these eleven.

08 · Deep dive 1 of 3 · the middle of the spectrum

RSSM and Dreamer: learn a small belief, then dream in it

The model

What it's trained to do

Gaussian form of the transition is the survey's Eq.10. DreamerV3 adds symlog targets, return normalisation, and KL balancing with "free bits", and trains the actor on 15-step imagined rollouts.

How I think about it

Two kinds of memory. is the certain part ("I pushed right twice, the cup should be near the edge"). is the uncertain part ("it might have tipped"). When a frame arrives the model looks; when it's dreaming it has to guess, and the KL term trains the guess to match the look.

Seven years of one idea

What it's good at

Thousands of imagined rollouts are cheap because they're small vectors. It's proven on real hardware: DayDreamer taught a real quadruped to roll over, stand and walk in one hour with no simulator.

Where it struggles

A Gaussian pixel decoder blurs small details, and a GRU forgets over long horizons. That's why Dreamer 4 moved to a transformer world model.

Talk notes
Toggle the two modes on the diagram. Training: real frame comes in from the bottom, posterior z uses it. Imagining: no frame, prior ẑ is sampled, and the actor learns from rewards it imagines. If asked "how far ahead does it dream": about 15 steps in DreamerV3, then a learned value estimates the rest.
09 · Deep dive 2 of 3 · the left of the spectrum

Diffusion world models: draw the next frame from noise

Generating one frame of the cup scene. Drag to change the number of denoising steps.
denoising steps3

Start from pure noise. A network looks at the last few frames and the chosen action and removes a bit of noise. Repeat times and we have the next frame. Append it to the history, take the next action, and do it again.

Survey Eq.14: learn to predict the noise that was added to the true next frame at noise level .

Three engineering ideas that made it work

Enough steps to pick one future

When the opponent could move left or right, one denoising step averages both into a blur; more steps commit to one. DIAMOND switched from DDPM to EDM and got stable rollouts with just 3 steps per frame.

Train on a slightly wrong past

GameNGen adds noise to the context frames during training, so at test time the model can recover from its own small mistakes. That kept DOOM stable for minutes.

Different noise per frame

Diffusion Forcing gives each token its own noise level, so near frames can be sharp while far ones stay fuzzy: a bridge between next-token prediction and full-video diffusion.

Results that put it on the map

DIAMOND: 1.46 human-normalised score on Atari 100k, the best for an agent trained only inside a world model, plus a playable CS:GO. GameNGen: DOOM at 20 fps on one TPU, and raters only slightly better than chance at spotting fakes. The same recipe at scale became Sora, Genie 2/3 and Cosmos (trained on 20M+ hours of video).

The catch

It's slow (many network passes per frame), errors still accumulate, and looking right isn't the same as being right. Kang et al. found video models generalise "case-based", by nearest training example, rather than by learning physical rules.

Talk notes
Drag the slider to 1 and say: this is why early models were blurry, the model averages possible futures. At 3 it's sharp. Then the three tricks: enough steps, noisy context, per-frame noise. Finish on the catch: this family is the one most exposed to "looks real, isn't physical".
10 · Deep dive 3 of 3 · the right of the spectrum

JEPA: predict the meaning of the future, never draw it

LeCun's argument: predicting every pixel wastes capacity on leaves and lighting. Predict the next frame's embedding instead, and compare embeddings.

Stage 1 of V-JEPA 2. Numbers from the survey.

Survey Eq.11: predictor plus an exponential-moving-average target encoder; the loss lives entirely in representation space. sg = stop-gradient.

The trap: collapse

If both encoders output the same constant for everything, the loss is zero and nothing is learned. The slow EMA target and stop-gradient prevent this in I-JEPA and V-JEPA. LeWM (Mar 2026) uses a regulariser, SIGReg, that pushes embeddings toward an isotropic Gaussian, and is the first JEPA to train stably end-to-end from raw pixels.

Stage 2: add actions, then plan

Freeze the encoder, train a small action-conditioned predictor on under 62 hours of unlabeled robot video, and the arm can plan zero-shot in two different labs. Planning is just a search for the push sequence whose imagined end looks most like a photo of the goal:

The search, live: cross-entropy method (CEM). Sample 80 push sequences (grey), keep the 10 best (blue), refit the distribution (green = its mean), repeat. Execute the first push, then replan.
Toy 2D illustration of the planner, not V-JEPA 2's real data. In the real system each "dot" is a predicted embedding, and the cost is measured to the goal image's embedding.

Why people like it

Cheap: V-JEPA 2-AC needs 16 s per action where planning with Cosmos took 4 minutes. The same features also do video understanding. The survey calls it a shift from "generative" to "predictive" learning.

What the 2026 diagnostics found

On LeWM, linear probes recover position (R² ≈ 0.95) but velocity sits at chance. V-JEPA 2's predictor loses a moving hidden object within 0.3 s. These latents know where things are better than how they move.

Talk notes
Analogy: a good driver doesn't picture every leaf, they predict "the car ahead will brake". Click "Next iteration" 3–4 times: the cloud of guesses tightens around a path that reaches the goal. Then the honest part: these models are great at "where", weak at "how fast", which matters for pushing a cup that slides.

Which family is measured by what, and why

● standard, ○ sometimes. My synthesis from the model papers and both surveys. The pattern: the architecture's output decides what can be measured. A model with no decoder can only be scored by task return; a video model gets scored on video quality, which says little about control.

The benchmarks that come up most

The perceptual-quality trap

FVD, the standard video metric, favours per-frame looks over motion: FVD can be halved just by picking static videos. Replacements like JEDi need only 16% of the samples and track human judgments of motion better.

Atari scores, done properly

Report the interquartile mean with bootstrap confidence intervals, because a mean is dominated by a few outlier games.

Talk notes
Key message: the architecture decides what we can measure, and most current benchmarks measure looks or open-loop prediction. The survey's practical proposal is a "benchmark card": before running, write down what result would refute the claim. If we evaluate world models ourselves, that's the habit to adopt.
13 · What's still broken

"Visually rich but physically blind"

That's the Shanghai roadmap's summary of the field. The Zidan survey ranks long-horizon consistency as the single biggest technical bottleneck. Try it:

Illustration, not data: the cup's true path (black) vs a model with a small systematic error per step (orange). Replanning re-anchors the rollout to a real observation.

Every prediction is built on the previous prediction, so small errors stack up. MBPO's analysis shows the gap between real and imagined return grows linearly with both rollout length and one-step error:

The survey's illustration: a 1% per-step error can diverge badly after tens of steps.

What people do about it: keep rollouts short and branch them from real data (MBPO), re-anchor to observations (MPC), add noise during training (GameNGen), give each frame its own noise level (Diffusion Forcing), or plan in chunks (Director, THICK). None of them removes the trade-off: longer imagination plans further but gives the planner more errors to exploit.

14 · Takeaways

Takeaways

1. A world model is a learned, action-conditioned simulator whose predictions are used to make decisions. The surveys agree on this definition.

2. The real design choice is what to predict: pixels (diffusion), a small belief (Dreamer), embeddings (JEPA), or only value (MuZero). Cost, interpretability and the right benchmark all follow from that choice.

3. The current phase is about action and unification: video models becoming robot policies, and single backbones that render, simulate and plan.

4. Errors compound over long horizons, and planners exploit them. This is still the biggest technical bottleneck.

5. Realistic video is not physical understanding, and most benchmarks never close the loop. Results should be judged by closed-loop, task-level evidence.
